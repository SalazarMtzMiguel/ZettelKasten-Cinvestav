This systematic literature review reveals a significant turning point in the field of design engineering regarding the level of automation achieved and the diversity of approaches employed. 


However, one of the most prominent is the use of AI, and more specifically LLMs in multi-agent systems, which has been widely adopted in recent years. This approach is frequently used as the core of Software Engineering 3.0 (SE 3.0), which is potentially capable of addressing all aspects of software design. 
     
   
   Additionally, they proactively contribute to the development of more efficient computational structures. This development signifies a substantial qualitative advancement, representing a transition to profound autonomy. Consequently, the future of self-design is predicated on decentralized, goal-driven collaboration, which entails the repositioning of the human developer from a manual coder to a high-level orchestrator of autonomous agents.
   
     
     
    
    Notwithstanding this encouraging trajectory, an examination of the existing literature indicates a conspicuous absence of consolidation. The self-design of systems, whether conceived as a partial or total process, is not governed by a single, unified theoretical framework. Instead, a highly heterogeneous, multifaceted approach has emerged, encompassing bio-inspired architectures, meta-heuristic search functions, and, more recently, Generative AI. However, a critical caveat must be strongly emphasized: the vast majority of the reviewed literature is strictly confined to conceptual architectures and theoretical frameworks. Given the dearth of tangible, real-scale implementations, successfully bridging the gap between theoretical models and real-world execution remains a formidable challenge for the research community.
    
    This arduous transition to real-world implementation faces severe barriers, highlighting a fundamental trade-off in the current state of the art. Historically, the strict prioritization of robustness and safety has restricted a system's ability to transcend its initial design boundaries, making true generative autonomy difficult to achieve safely. The efficacy of deep learning and LLMs is contingent upon access to substantial, unbiased, and high-quality datasets. In the domain of software engineering, such datasets remain critically scarce, often forcing reliance on synthetic data that fails to adequately represent real-world complexities. This data limitation, in conjunction with the intrinsic "black-box" nature of these technologies, substantially impedes the interpretability of—and confidence in—the architectural decisions made autonomously, thereby exacerbating the latent risk of algorithmic hallucinations and suboptimal design choices.
    
    Another significant challenge pertains to the validation of software design, which is inherently qualitative and multifaceted. In the domain of software engineering, a single problem may be addressed through a multitude of architectural perspectives that are considered valid. Conventional evaluation metrics frequently prove inadequate for capturing the nuances of complex engineering principles, the extent of innovation, or the practical viability of autonomously generated designs. Furthermore, the lack of universally accepted standards for formal specification languages complicates the mathematical verification and validation processes necessary to ensure that generated designs consistently meet rigorous security and performance criteria.
    
    In order to address this discrepancy between generative creativity and operational reliability, it is essential to reintegrate traditional engineering knowledge into these novel paradigms. The reusability of software, augmented by contemporary techniques such as process mining and exploration within vast heuristic search spaces, offers a sophisticated solution. The pragmatic analogy of "not reinventing the wheel" is applicable here: self-designing systems can leverage repositories of proven architectures and evolve from them rather than generating solutions from scratch.
    
    Moreover, the integration of rule engines, ontologies, and formal domain knowledge representations serves as a crucial deductive anchor for AI systems. While LLM agents exhibit inductive and generative fluidity, ontologies function as semantic "guardrails," ensuring that the system strictly adheres to business logic and architectural constraints.
    
    The synthesis of these findings indicates that mature self-design will not emerge from the elimination of traditional engineering controls; rather, it will emerge from the modernization of these controls. Achieving deep autonomy necessitates a delicate equilibrium: leveraging state-of-the-art generative capabilities for continuous invention while embedding robust, deliberative, and strongly typed regulatory frameworks to ensure that this evolution remains reliable, predictable, and deeply context-aware.
